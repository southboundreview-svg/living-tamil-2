History · 2024

A meditation centre's opening, and a festival that ran a day longer than planned.

The 2024 Vishnupuram festival widened into a multi-day gathering recognising a new generation of writers together, rather than a single laureate — translated and adapted from the original Tamil account.

A festival that grew by two days

The 2024 festival began a day earlier than usual and, by its end, had run a day longer than scheduled. Jeyamohan travelled from Chennai to Coimbatore on the evening of 19 December, arriving the next morning at Fortune Suites, near the Rajasthani Sangh auditorium that has hosted the gathering in past years.

A meditation centre opens

The morning of the 20th opened with an inauguration rather than a reading: at 8:30, a new centre of the Ananda Chaitanya Foundation opened its doors, with Jeyamohan and Arun Mozhi in attendance. Its director, Tillai Senthilprabhu, had left a career in technology to teach meditation; Jeyamohan encouraged him to return to that teaching through the Unified Wisdom network. The foundation's work spans three strands — educational scholarships, reading instruction and meditation practice — and Jeyamohan noted that its meditation classes, addressing the ordinary modern afflictions of scattered attention and poor sleep, had visibly changed hundreds of lives. Jeyamohan gave the inaugural remarks, and Maravinmainthan Muthaiah, a teacher of classical and Saivite literature, addressed the gathering.

Old friends, held close

By evening, the crowd had gathered at the Rajasthani Sangh auditorium, where the Meikkalam theatre group ran a final overnight rehearsal with lighting and projection. Among those present was the writer R. Murugan, who came despite living with Parkinson's disease and a recent hospitalisation — encouraged, by Jeyamohan's account, to keep writing daily regardless of how the pages came out. Jeyamohan visited him at his lodging during the festival, one of several quieter moments the larger programme made room for.

Readers and their writers

The heart of the festival remained its reader-writer sessions, each drawing crowds of roughly 500 and moderated throughout with warmth and humour by Rajagopalan. Mantramurthy Azhagu discussed literary activism with Vasagasalai Karthikeyan; Mayilan Chinnappan, Lavanya Sundararajan, Kayaal, Tendral, Chitran and Thamizh Praba each sat for extended conversation on their own published work rather than general literary themes. Jeyamohan himself put two of the questions from the floor — one on the influence of the writer Bashir on Keeranur Jageeraja, another on the regional musical traditions running through Thamizh Praba's fiction.

Two plays, minimally staged

The evening's entertainment came from the Meikkalam theatre group, who staged two short plays — a twenty-minute sword-play and a forty-minute piece titled Kazhumadan — built deliberately around bare staging rather than spectacle. Jeyamohan's own view, offered afterward, was that strong theatre for a literary audience depends on the quality of the writing behind it as much as the performance; both pieces drew visibly strong reactions through the evening and in conversation afterward.

Fifteen years on

The shared meals remained, as in earlier years, a small but central part of the gathering. Arangasamy, present at the very first festival in 2010, was seen moved to see how large the communal table had grown — a feature visiting writers, among them Vivek Shanbhag, singled out as unlike any other literary festival they knew in India. After the closing ceremony and the customary group photograph, one group of student volunteers set off on a 400-kilometre, Gandhi-inspired salt march from Erode to Vedaranyam, travelling without money and relying on local hospitality along the way, while others dispersed to a film festival in Thiruvananthapuram, to Thanjavur, and to pilgrimage sites further afield.

Reflecting on fifteen years of the festival, Jeyamohan attributed its persistence not to any organisational hierarchy but to a simple, deliberately unglamorous formula — small in scale, always being refined, and carried forward "one flame lighting the next," each participant contributing according to what they were able to give.
