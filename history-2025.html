History · 2025

Seven hundred readers, and five young writers honoured for the first time.

The 2025 Vishnupuram festival drew its largest crowd yet, families included, and introduced a new honour — the Ramesh Preethan Memorial Award — for five emerging Tamil writers — translated and adapted from the original Tamil account.

A festival that keeps growing

Held over 20–22 December 2025 in Coimbatore, the festival drew around 700 attendees — some 500 seated inside the hall and another 50 standing outside on its fullest morning. Jeyamohan noted a shift he had watched build for several years: whole families now attended together, women who had first come to reading through the Unified Wisdom meditation classes had grown into some of the festival's most serious readers, and children were present throughout the venue — enough that organisers began discussing dedicated programming for them next year.

Publishers and novelists

The first session paired two publishers with very different missions — Jeeva Karikalan, who publishes contemporary Tamil writers, and Azhisai Srinivasan, who specialises in rediscovering and reprinting older, out-of-print texts. A second session brought together the fiction writers Guna Kandasamy, questioned on the tension between his birthplace and the places his fiction actually inhabits; Arisankar, a Puducherry writer who named the older novelist Prabhanjan as his literary predecessor, recalling childhood encounters with him at a neighbourhood betel-leaf shop; and Kulasekharan, whose work sits close to grassroots environmental activism, and who was pressed on whether his fiction is built, structurally, around the defeat of the causes it depicts.

Dalit writing, translation, and a Telugu guest

The activist, poet and essayist Yaazhan Aadi spoke to the pull between writing as a Dalit and the wish, as a writer, to move beyond any single fixed identity. The poet and translator Anuradha Anand examined what makes a poem translatable at all, and how such choices get made. A late addition to the programme, the Telugu writer Madhurantakam Narendra — standing in for a translator unable to travel — argued that Tamil Nadu and the Telugu-speaking states share not just cultural ground but, in his words, "the same linguistic space," while regretting how little Telugu literature travels beyond its own region. His own father had translated the Tamil novelist Jayakanthan into Telugu; Narendra himself has written novels on the singer M. S. Subbulakshmi and on the Udumalaipettai countryside.

The Ramesh Preethan Memorial Award

The festival's central ceremony, on the afternoon of the second day, introduced a new honour established in memory of the writer Ramesh Preethan: five young writers were recognised, each preceded by a short video introduction. Saju, a researcher and poet documenting Tamil folk performance traditions, was recognised alongside Selvakumar Pechimuthu, whose recent novella Kalimaadam won the Seer Vaadhaer Circle award; Devi Lingam, whose Neruppu Odu examines women's lived experience and who has since published a further story collection; the translator Azhagiya Manavalan, whose Tamil rendering of P. K. Balakrishnan's writing on the craft of the novel found a wide readership; and the writer Ashok Ram Raj. Rajagopalan presented the writers' work with characteristic humour, Senthil Kumar read the citations, and Nanjil Nadan and the Kannada writer Jayant Kaikini presented the award shields, each bearing Ramesh Preethan's image.

A 45-minute documentary on Ramesh Preethan, Ammaiappan Ayonikan, made by the writer Akaramuthalvan, screened before a hall so full its doors had to be closed. Akaramuthalvan, who had encouraged Preethan's own turn toward Tamil nationalism late in his life, spoke afterward of how Preethan himself had asked for the film to be made. Jeyamohan, addressing the five new laureates directly, reflected on the double edge of early recognition — how the visibility it brings can just as easily curdle into later disappointment if a young writer doesn't grow to meet it, a caution he said the writer Vengat Samynathan had once offered him at the same age.

The evening

The main evening event opened with a recitation from Kambaramayanam by Rajan Somasundaram, director of the American chapter of the Vishnupuram Literary Circle. K. V. Arangasamy retold the organisation's own founding story — a first meeting between Jeyamohan and the Arangasamy family in Nagercoil back in 2009. Jayant Kaikini, drawing on his years hosting the Kannada television programme Namaskaara, gave what Jeyamohan called the evening's finest address, ranging across literature's restlessness and the nature of real intellectual engagement. Madhurantakam Narendra, fighting a bad cold in weather that had dropped to 17°C, managed only a few words. Closing remarks came from Dynamic Nadarajan, who — in a small gesture particular to Vishnupuram's evenings — thanked each participant individually by name rather than as a group.

Jeyamohan closed the festival by returning to a principle he keeps returning to: that these sessions are deliberately never filmed, so that their intimacy survives — a stance he holds as one form of literature's resistance to a culture of constant video documentation. The gathering broke up after midnight, with a final photograph taken of writers, readers and volunteers from across India, the United States and Australia — Jeyamohan noting, as he often does, that what matters most is disappearing into that gathering rather than standing apart from it.
